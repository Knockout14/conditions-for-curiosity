Conditions for Curiosity

Question Night: Your Part

For the adult who asks. Kids 3 to 8.

A question you both answer is a conversation, not a quiz. Here's how to keep it one.

About 5 minutes. Optional, and nothing is graded.

You'll practice

  1. Answering first.
  2. Sharing, not explaining or quizzing.
  3. "I don't know," a surprise, or silence.
  4. The tap at the end of the night.

The one rule

You answer too, and you go first.

You're finding out how they think, not teaching.

Why it's built this way

You can't quiz someone while you're sharing something of your own. And when you go first and say you're unsure, your kid sees that not knowing is allowed.

Working model

Not a proven result. The app hasn't been tested as a product yet.

Read more (optional)

When adults are told to teach

In one preregistered experiment (King et al., 2023), mothers of six-month-old babies who were asked to teach their baby something became more intrusive while they played. That study is about infants, so applying it to kids 3 to 8 is an extension of the finding, not part of it.

When adults are told only to watch

In the same study, mothers asked to learn from their baby were rated as less warm. We read that as a reason to share something of yourself, and not only to watch. That reading is ours, and it is untested.

Taking turns

In one study of parents and children aged 8 to 13 (Brummelman et al., 2024), children felt more loved in the moment when the parent started a turn-taking exchange of sharing about themselves, compared with small talk. The task and the ages were different from this app's.

Wondering out loud

In one experiment Engel (2011) describes, 8- and 9-year-olds did a science activity with an adult. When she said something like "I wonder what would happen if..." before stepping out, children were much more likely to keep exploring on their own.

Finding out or teaching?

Same question, six answers.

Answer it yourself first, in your head or out loud. Ten seconds.

Where do you think puddles go when the sun comes out?

Finding out leaves the question open. Teaching closes it with an answer, a word to repeat, or a quiz. Sort each answer by how it sounds, not by whether it's right.

1 of 6

"I think the water goes up into the air, but I'm not sure. Yesterday a puddle by my bus stop got smaller and I couldn't tell when."

Finding out. A guess, an honest "not sure," and one real detail. It may even be right, and it still stays open.

"It soaks into the ground. That's where all puddles go."

Teaching. A flat "that's it" closes the question, even if the fact is right.

"When I was little I thought the sun drank them. Now I honestly don't know. Maybe some goes up and some soaks in."

Finding out. A memory, an honest "I don't know," and a loose guess.

"The heat of the sun turns the water into vapor. It's called evaporation. Say it with me."

Teaching. It hands over a word to say back. A quiz in a friendly voice.

"Not sure. I notice they disappear faster on sunny days, but I can't say what that tells me."

Finding out. Something noticed, and an honest stop. Short is fine.

"Remember the water cycle? Water goes up, makes clouds and comes back as rain. So where do puddles come from?"

Teaching. A recap, then a question with a right answer. A quiz, even when it's kind.

What to listen for

Finding out: a guess, a memory, an honest "not sure," a real detail. Nothing to repeat back.

Teaching: a flat "that's it," a word to say back, or a question with a right answer.

You don't have to fake not knowing. Say what you think, once, and leave it open.

Moment 1 of 3: "I don't know"

You asked, "What do you think the moon is made of?" and answered first. Your kid, 5 or 6, shrugs: "I don't know."

What do you do next?

Tap the others to see what each one does.

That's the explaining reflex. It rescues them, and it ends the wondering.

A curious next move. You took "I don't know" as a real answer and gave an honest one back. They can add something now, later, or never.

That's the quiz reflex. A guess on demand feels like a test.

Moment 2 of 3: A surprising answer

You asked, "What do you think clouds are made of?" and answered first. Your kid, 7 or 8, says, "Cotton. Like a giant pillow."

What do you do next?

Tap the others to see what each one does.

A curious next move. You stayed with their picture. Whether it's right can wait. If it matters, it will come up again.

That's the correcting reflex. It's well meant, and it turns their idea into a mistake.

That's the quiz reflex. It sounds playful, but they'll hear that their idea was the wrong one.

Moment 3 of 3: Silence

You asked, "Where do you think shadows go at night?" and answered first. Your kid, 5 or 6, goes quiet and looks at the ceiling. A few seconds pass.

What do you do next?

Tap the others to see what each one does.

That's the explaining reflex, in disguise. A hint steers them toward your answer. The quiet was theirs.

That's the quiz reflex. Two choices, one of them right, turn wondering into a multiple-choice test.

A curious next move. Quiet is often thinking. If it's still quiet after ten, you can say, "That's a thinking one. We can come back to it."

In the moment

Don't explain, quiz, or correct.

Make the smaller, curious move instead.

All three come from caring and habit. Notice the reach, and let it pass.

After the question

When you've both answered, the night is nearly done.

Afterward. You'll see what you wrote. You don't need to write down what they said.

Why this one works. A short note on the question. Open it or skip it.

One tap. "Did your child ask a question back?" Yes or No, plus an optional note on what they said.

What the app keeps

The app keeps your answer, your tap and your note. It doesn't score them, and it doesn't turn them into a report or a trend for you.

No is a fine answer. It isn't a bad night, or a grade on you or your kid.

Then you're done. Close the app.

Your first week

One pick. Then ask when it fits.

  1. Pick. Once a week, put three questions in the order you like. One swap a week if one doesn't fit.
  2. Answer first. Type or speak your answer before you ask.
  3. Ask. Say the question as written.
  4. Tap and stop. One tap, an optional note, done. About 90 seconds.

Two or three nights a week is plenty. Miss a week and nothing is lost. There are no streaks.
