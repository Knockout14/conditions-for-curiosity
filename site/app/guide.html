Conditions for Curiosity

Question Night: Your Part

A short guide for the adult who asks. Parents, caregivers and teachers of kids 3 to 8.

This takes about 7 minutes. It is optional, and the app doesn't wait on it. Nothing here is graded.

What you'll practice

  1. Why you answer first, and why that isn't the same as giving the right answer.
  2. Telling the difference between sharing what you think and explaining or quizzing.
  3. A curious next move when they say "I don't know," say something surprising, or go quiet.
  4. What the tap at the end of the night is, and why "No" is fine.

The one rule

You answer too, and you go first.

You're finding out how they think, not teaching.

Answering first, honestly, is the whole idea. The rest of this course is practice.

Why it's built this way

A question you also answer can't become a quiz.

Two ideas sit behind the design.

First, a question you answer too can't turn into an interrogation. You can't quiz someone while you're sharing something of your own.

Second, when you go first and say something unsure, you show your kid that not knowing is allowed here.

Working model

This is a working model, not a proven result. We haven't tested the app as a product yet.

Read more (optional)

When adults are told to teach

In one preregistered experiment (King et al., 2023), mothers of six-month-old babies who were asked to teach their baby something became more intrusive while they played. That study is about infants, so applying it to kids 3 to 8 is an extension of the finding, not part of it.

When adults are told only to watch

In the same study, mothers asked to learn from their baby were rated as less warm. We read that as a reason to share something of yourself, and not only to watch. That reading is ours, and it is untested.

Taking turns

In one study of parents and children aged 8 to 13 (Brummelman et al., 2024), children felt more loved in the moment when the parent started a turn-taking exchange of sharing about themselves, compared with small talk. The task and the ages were different from this app's.

Wondering out loud

In one experiment Engel (2011) describes, 8- and 9-year-olds did a science activity with an adult. When she said something like "I wonder what would happen if..." before stepping out, children were much more likely to keep exploring on their own.

Finding out or teaching?

Same question, six answers.

First, answer it yourself, in your head or out loud. Take ten seconds.

Where do you think puddles go when the sun comes out?

Now sort six adult answers to the same question. Go by how it sounds, not by whether it's right.

1 of 6

"I think the water goes up into the air, but I've never actually watched it happen, so I'm not sure. Yesterday a puddle by my bus stop got smaller and I couldn't tell when."

Finding out. It makes a guess, says it's unsure, and shares one real detail. The guess may even be right. It still sounds like finding out, because it stays open.

"It soaks into the ground. That's where all puddles go."

Teaching. It closes the question with a flat "that's it." The tone gives it away, not whether the fact is right.

"When I was little I thought the sun drank them. Now I honestly don't know. I'd guess it's some mix of up into the air and down into the ground."

Finding out. A memory, an honest "I don't know," and a loose guess. Nothing to repeat back.

"The heat of the sun turns the water into vapor. It's called evaporation. Say it with me."

Teaching. The facts may be fine, but it hands over a word and asks them to say it back. That's a quiz in a friendly voice.

"Not sure. I notice they disappear faster on sunny days, but I can't say what that tells me."

Finding out. Short, shares something noticed, and admits where the thinking stops. Short is fine.

"Remember the water cycle? Water goes up, makes clouds and comes back as rain. So where do puddles come from?"

Teaching. It recaps a lesson and ends with a question that has a right answer. That's a quiz, even when it's kind.

What to listen for

Finding out sounds like: a guess or a memory, an honest "I'm not sure," a real detail from your own day, and nothing for them to repeat back.

Teaching sounds like: a flat "that's it," a new word to say back, or a question with a right answer on the end.

A correct fact can still sound like teaching. And you don't have to fake not knowing. Say what you think, once, and leave it open.

Moment 1 of 3: "I don't know"

You asked, "What do you think the moon is made of?" You answered first. Your kid, 5 or 6 years old, shrugs and says, "I don't know."

What do you do next?

That's the explaining reflex. It rescues them from not knowing, and it also ends the wondering. You've moved from finding out to teaching.

A curious next move. You took "I don't know" as a real answer and were honest about your own. They don't owe you anything more. They can add something now, later, or never.

That's the quiz reflex. A guess on demand feels like a test, and "I don't know" was an honest answer.

Moment 2 of 3: A surprising answer

You asked, "What do you think clouds are made of?" You answered first. Your kid, 7 or 8 years old, says, "Cotton. Like a giant pillow."

What do you do next?

A curious next move. You stayed with their picture instead of checking it. Whether it's right can wait. If it matters, it will come up again.

That's the correcting reflex. It's quick and well meant, and it turns their idea into a mistake. Question night isn't the place to fix it.

That's the quiz reflex. It sounds playful, but it has a right answer waiting at the end. They'll hear that their idea was the wrong one.

Moment 3 of 3: Silence

You asked, "Where do you think shadows go at night?" You answered first. Your kid, 5 or 6 years old, goes quiet and looks up at the ceiling. A few seconds pass.

What do you do next?

That's the explaining reflex, in disguise. A hint steers them toward the answer you have in mind. The quiet was theirs to use.

That's the quiz reflex. Two choices, with one of them likely being right, turn a wondering question into a multiple-choice test.

A curious next move. Quiet is often thinking. If it's still quiet after ten, you can say, "That's a thinking one. We can come back to it."

In the moment

Don't explain, quiz, or correct.

Make the smaller, curious move instead.

All three come from caring, and most of us reach for one out of habit. Notice the reach, and let it pass.

After the question

When you've both answered, the night is nearly done.

Afterward. You'll see what you wrote. Their turn is theirs. You don't need to write down what they said unless you want to.

Why this one works. A short note, closed by default. It only appears once you've both answered. Open it if you like, and skip it if you don't.

One tap. The app asks one thing: "Did your child ask a question back?" You tap Yes or No. There's an optional note for a word or a sentence of what they said, if you want to keep it. It's fine to skip.

What the app keeps

The app keeps your answer, your tap and your note. It doesn't score them, and it doesn't turn them into a report or a trend for you.

No is a fine answer. It isn't a bad night. It isn't a grade on you or on your kid.

Then you're done. There's no streak to keep, and nothing is lost by skipping a night.

Your first week

One pick. Then ask when it fits.

  1. Pick. Once a week, the app shows three questions. Put them in the order you like. Not feeling one? You get one swap a week.
  2. Answer first. Type (or speak) your answer in your own words before you turn to your kid.
  3. Ask. Say the question just as written. A question, not a quiz. Nothing else appears until you've both gone.
  4. Tap and stop. One tap, an optional note, done. It takes about 90 seconds.

Two or three nights in a week is plenty. If a week gets away from you, nothing is lost. There are no streaks to keep.
